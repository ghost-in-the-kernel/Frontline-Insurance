High-Value Home Insurance: What Homeowners Need to Know

Your home is more than just a place to live. For many homeowners, it’s one of their largest financial investments and may include custom features, premium materials, or valuable personal belongings that require specialized insurance considerations. 

If your home has a higher rebuilding cost than a typical residence, a standard homeowners insurance policy may not provide the level of protection you need. Understanding how high-value home insurance works can help you choose coverage that’s appropriate for your property. 

What Is High-Value Home Insurance? 

High-value home insurance is designed for homes with higher rebuilding costs and unique insurance needs. 

There is no universal definition of a high-value home. Insurance companies generally consider factors such as a home’s replacement cost, custom construction, premium materials, architectural features, and valuable personal property when determining whether specialized coverage may be appropriate. 

It’s also important to understand that replacement cost is different from market value. 

Market value is what your home could sell for based on the local real estate market and includes the value of the land. 
Replacement cost is the estimated amount it would cost to rebuild your home using similar materials and craftsmanship after a covered loss. 

Depending on your home’s location, age, construction, and custom features, replacement cost may be higher or lower than its market value. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) recommends homeowners insure their homes based on replacement cost rather than market value, since rebuilding expenses don’t necessarily reflect real estate prices. 

Source: National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC), Replacement Cost vs. Actual Cash Value 
https://content.naic.org/article/whats-difference-between-actual-cash-value-coverage-and-replacement-cost-coverage  

Who Should Consider High-Value Home Insurance? 

High-value home insurance may be appropriate for homeowners who own: 

  • Luxury or estate homes 
  • Custom-built or architect-designed homes 
  • Homes with premium finishes or specialty materials 
  • Historic homes requiring specialized repairs 
  • Homes with valuable collections such as artwork, jewelry, wine, antiques, or collectibles 

Every property is different. An insurance professional can help determine whether specialized coverage may be appropriate for your home and individual insurance needs. 

How to Choose the Right High-Value Home Insurance 

Finding the right insurance for a high-value home involves more than comparing premiums. Consider the insurer’s financial strength, available coverage options, claims experience, and expertise with higher-value properties. 

1. Research Insurance Companies 

Before selecting an insurer, take time to evaluate their reputation and financial stability. 

Consider reviewing: 

  • Financial strength ratings 
  • Customer satisfaction studies 
  • Better Business Bureau (BBB) ratings 
  • Online customer reviews 
  • Coverage options and policy features 

The NAIC recommends comparing multiple insurers, understanding coverage options, reviewing deductibles, and asking questions before purchasing a homeowners insurance policy. 

2. Ask for Recommendations 

Friends, family members, or trusted professionals who own similar homes may be able to recommend insurance companies or agents they have had positive experiences with. 

Personal recommendations can complement your own research when evaluating insurance providers. 

3. Work With an Independent Insurance Agent 

Independent insurance agents represent multiple insurance companies rather than just one. 

Because they can compare policies from different carriers, they may be able to help you evaluate coverage options that fit your home’s replacement cost, personal property, and insurance needs. 

4. Understand Your Coverage Options 

Not all homeowners insurance policies provide the same level of protection. 

Depending on the insurer and policy, additional coverage options may include: 

  • Higher personal property limits 
  • Increased liability limits 
  • Replacement cost coverage for personal belongings, when available 
  • Scheduled personal property for valuable items 
  • Additional living expense coverage following a covered loss 

Review your policy carefully to understand what is covered, applicable limits, deductibles, exclusions, and optional endorsements. Coverage varies by insurer and policy. 

Don’t Overlook Valuable Personal Property 

Many high-value homeowners own belongings that may exceed the standard limits included in a homeowners insurance policy. 

Examples include: 

  • Fine jewelry 
  • Watches 
  • Artwork 
  • Wine collections 
  • Musical instruments 
  • Antiques 
  • Collectibles 

If you own valuable items, ask your insurance professional whether additional coverage or scheduled personal property may be appropriate. 

Create a Home Inventory 

A home inventory is one of the easiest ways to prepare for a future insurance claim. 

Consider: 

  • Photographing valuable belongings 
  • Saving receipts when available 
  • Recording serial numbers for electronics and appliances 
  • Keeping an updated list of major household items and their estimated value 

The NAIC recommends maintaining a home inventory because it can help document your belongings and simplify the claims process after a covered loss. 

Source: National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC), Homeowners Insurance Consumer Guide 
https://content.naic.org/consumer/homeowners-insurance 

Frequently Asked Questions 

What is considered a high-value home? 
There is no universal dollar amount that defines a high-value home. Insurance companies may consider factors such as your home’s replacement cost, custom features, construction materials, and valuable personal property when determining whether specialized coverage is appropriate. 

Is replacement cost the same as market value? 
No. Market value reflects what your home could sell for, while replacement cost estimates what it would cost to rebuild your home after a covered loss using similar materials and workmanship. 

Does standard homeowners insurance cover luxury homes? 
It may. However, homes with higher replacement costs or unique features may benefit from specialized coverage options or higher policy limits. Coverage varies by insurer and policy. 

Do I need additional coverage for jewelry or artwork? 
Possibly. Standard homeowners insurance policies often include coverage limits for certain categories of valuable personal property. Additional coverage may be available depending on your insurer and policy. 

Protecting What Matters Most 

Insuring a high-value home requires careful consideration of your property’s unique characteristics and insurance needs. Whether your home features custom construction, premium materials, or valuable personal belongings, understanding your coverage options can help you make informed decisions about protecting your investment. 

Before purchasing or renewing your homeowners insurance, review your policy with your insurance professional to ensure your coverage aligns with your home’s replacement cost, valuable personal property, and individual needs.

What Happens After You File a Homeowners Insurance Claim? A Step-by-Step Guide

Filing a homeowners insurance claim can feel overwhelming, especially if you’ve never experienced property damage before. Understanding what typically happens during the claims process can help you know what to expect and how to prepare. 

While every claim is unique, most claims follow a similar process – from reporting the loss to documenting damage and communicating with your adjuster. 

This guide explains the general steps homeowners can expect after filing a property insurance claim. 

Step 1: Report the Loss 

The claims process begins when you report damage to your insurance company.  

When reporting a claim, you may be asked to provide information such as: 

  • The date the damage occurred (if known) 
  • A description of what happened  
  • The location of the damage 
  • Photos or videos, if available 

Providing accurate information helps your claim move through the review process more efficiently.  

Step 2: Your Claim is Assigned 

After your claim is reported, it is generally assigned to a claims professional who will review the information provided.  

Your adjuster may contact you to: 

  • Discuss reported damage 
  • Ask follow-up questions 
  • Explain the next steps 
  • Answer questions about the claims process 

Keeping your contact information current can help avoid communication delays.  

Step 3: An Inspection May Be Scheduled 

Depending on the type of loss, an inspection of the property may be needed as part of the claims process.  

During an inspection, the adjuster or another qualified representative may: 

  • Review the reported damage 
  • Document affected areas 
  • Take measurements or photographs 
  • Gather information needed to evaluate the claim 

Not every claim requires the same type of inspection.  

Step 4: Document the Damage 

One of the most helpful things homeowners can do is document the damage before cleanup or repairs begin, when it is safe to do so.  

Helpful documentation may include: 

  • Photographs 
  • Videos 
  • Receipts Repair estimates 
  • Records of emergency services performed 

Keeping organized records may help support the claims review process.  

Step 5: Protect Your Property from Further Damage 

If it is safe to do so, homeowners should take reasonable steps to help prevent additional damage after a loss. Your safety should always come first, and you should never attempt repairs or mitigation activities that could put you at risk.  

If you’re unsure how to safely protect your property or what temporary measures may be appropriate, contact your claims representative for guidance.  

Be sure to document any additional damage that occurs and keep receipts for any emergency measures you take, when applicable.  

Step 6: Before Hiring Restoration or Mitigation Services 

Following property damage, homeowners may be contacted by restoration or mitigation companies offering immediate services.  

Before authorizing repairs or signing agreements, it’s important to understand how those services may relate to your claim.  

Depending on your policy, certain conditions, limitations, or authorization requirements may apply.  

If you have any questions about repairs, mitigation services, or documentation requirements, contact your claims representative before signing contracts whenever possible.  

Step 7: Keep Damaged Property When Possible 

Unless there is a safety concern or you’re otherwise instructed, avoid disposing of damaged items until your insurance company has had an opportunity to inspect them.  

If you’re unsure whether something should be discarded, ask your claims representative before disposal.  

Step 8: Submit Requested Information Promptly 

Your adjuster may request additional information while reviewing your claim.  

This could include: 

  • Photographs 
  • Receipts 
  • Proof of ownership 
  • Repair estimates 
  • Contractor information 
  • Additional documentation related to the reported loss 

Responding promptly may help avoid unnecessary delays during the review process.  

Frequently Asked Questions 

How long does a homeowners insurance claim take? 
Every claim is different. The timeline depends on factors such as the type of damage, the complexity of the claim, inspections, and the information needed to complete the review.  

Will my property need to be inspected? 
Some claims require an inspection, while others may not. The need for an inspection depends on the circumstances of the reported loss.  

Should I make temporary repairs? 
Protecting your property after a loss may be appropriate in some situations, but the right course of action depends on the circumstances. If you have questions about protecting your property or authorizing repairs or services, contact your claims representative for guidance before proceeding whenever possible.  

Should I throw away damaged items? 
When possible, keep damaged property available for inspection until you’ve received guidance from your claims representative, unless the items pose a health or safety risk.  

What documents should I keep? 
Keep photographs, receipts, repair estimates, invoices, and other records related to the damage and any emergency services performed.  

Tips for a Smoother Claims Experience 

While every claim is different, these best practices can help the process go more smoothly: 

  • Report damage as soon as reasonably possible 
  • Take clear photos before cleanup begins, when safe 
  • Protect your property from additional damage 
  • Keep receipts and important documentation 
  • Respond promptly to requests for information 
  • Ask questions if you’re unsure about the next step 

We’re Here to Help 

Experiencing property damage can be stressful, but understanding the claims process can help you feel more prepared. 

From the initial report through inspections and documentation, staying organized and communicating with your claims representative can help support the review process. Every claim is unique, and the specific steps may vary depending on the circumstances of the reported loss.

Fall Home Maintenance: Prepare your Home for the Season Ahead

Fall looks different for homeowners living on the Florida coast than for those living in the Tennessee mountains.  Some homeowners are watching for the first freeze, while others are still preparing for heavy rain and late-season storms. Wherever you live, this is a good time to check the parts of your home that protect it from water, wind, and cold. The Weather Channel's fall maintenance checklist is a useful starting point. Here are six areas to focus on as you prepare your home for the months ahead.  

Keep Water Moving Away from your Home 

Clear leaves and debris from gutters and downspouts, then check that water flows away from the foundation. Look for loose gutters, standing water, and drainage paths blocked by landscaping. While you are outside, scan the roof from the ground for missing or damaged shingles. Have a qualified professional inspect anything that needs a closer look.  

Check Doors, Windows, and Exposed Pipes 

Worn caulk or weatherstripping can let drafts and rain enter around doors and windows. Repair gaps you find and check less visible areas, such as the attic or crawl space, for signs of moisture.  

If freezing temperatures are possible where you live, disconnect and drain garden hoses before the first freeze. Identify pipes in unheated areas that may need insulation. The U.S. Department of Energy identifies air sealing and insulation as ways to improve a home's energy efficiency.  

Prepare your Heating System 

Schedule heating equipment service before you need it regularly. Replace filters according to the manufacturer's instructions and test the system early enough to address unusual sounds or poor airflow. If you use a fireplace, have the chimney inspected and cleaned by a professional.  

The U.S. Fire Administration recommends annual professional maintenance for heating equipment and chimneys. It also advises keeping anything that can burn at least three feet from heat sources.  

Test your Alarms 

Test smoke alarms and carbon monoxide detectors and replace batteries or devices as directed by the manufacturer. This is especially important in homes with fuel-burning appliances or fireplaces. The CDC's carbon monoxide guidance recommends detectors near sleeping areas and annual service for fuel-burning heating equipment.  

Look Over the Yard and Outdoor Spaces 

Check for dead or damaged branches near the home and arrange for professional trimming where needed. Secure outdoor furniture and decorations when high winds are forecast. Before temperatures drop, inspect walkways, and steps for cracks or uneven surfaces that could become harder to navigate in wet or icy weather.  

Make a Plan for Changing Weather 

Review the forecast for your area and keep essential supplies accessible. Know where to find your home's main water shutoff and make a plan for your household if severe weather disrupts power or travel.  

A fall home check does not need to happen all at once. Start with water drainage, heating and safety equipment, then schedule repairs that require a professional. Taking care of small issues now can help your home face the next season with confidence.

Why Lightning Damage Matters for Homeowners

Lightning can strike with little warning, and the damage it leaves behind can extend far beyond the initial storm. From damaged roofs and electrical systems to power surges that affect appliances and electronics, a single lightning strike has the potential to impact multiple areas of your home. According to the National Weather Service, lightning is one of the leading weather-related causes of injury and can generate temperatures hotter than the surface of the sun, making it a powerful force capable of causing significant property damage. The Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) also reports that insurers paid an estimated $1.65 billion in lightning-related homeowners insurance claims in 2025, with Florida consistently ranking among the states with the highest number of claims. Understanding how lightning can affect your home, and what your homeowners insurance policy may cover, can help you prepare before severe weather occurs. 

Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Lightning Damage? 

In many cases, homeowners insurance may provide coverage for damage caused by a lightning strike because it is generally considered a sudden and accidental event.  

Depending on your policy and the specific circumstances of the loss, coverage may apply to damage caused directly by lightning, such as fire damage or damage to parts of your home. Some policies may also provide coverage for certain power surge damage when the surge is the direct result of a lightning strike. Coverage is subject to your individual policy terms, conditions, deductibles, limitations, and exclusions.  

Reviewing your homeowners policy before storm season can help you better understand the coverage available to you.  

What Types of Damage Can Lightning Cause? 

A lightning strike doesn’t have to leave a visible hole in your roof to cause significant damage. In some cases, the effects may be immediate, while others may not become apparent until later.  

Roof Damage 

A direct lightning strike can damage roofing materials, flashing gutters, vents, or other exterior components. In some situations, it may also create openings that allow water intrusion if not addressed promptly.  

Even if your roof appears undamaged from the ground, it’s important to document anything unusual you notice after a severe storm.  

Electrical System Damage 

Lightning carries an enormous amount of electrical energy. A strike can damage: 

  • Electrical wiring 
  • Circuit breakers 
  • Electrical panels 
  • Outlets and switches 
  • HVAC system controls 
  • Garage door openers 

Some electrical damage may not be immediately visible, so homeowners should exercise caution if they suspect their home was struck. 

Appliances and Electronics 

Power surges caused by nearby lightning strikes may damage: 

  • Televisions 
  • Computers 
  • Refrigerators 
  • Internet equipment 
  • Smart home devices 
  • Washing machines 
  • Other household electronics 

Using surge protection can help reduce the risk of damage to certain electronics, although no system can eliminate every risk. 

Fire Damage 

One of the most serious consequences of a lightning strike is fire.  

Lightning can ignite roofing materials, attic spaces, siding, trees, or other combustible materials. If you smell smoke, see flames, or suspect a fire, leave the home immediately and call 911. 

The National Weather Service recommends treating every lightning strike seriously, even if damage is not immediately visible. 

Can Lightning Damage Your Roof Without Obvious Signs? 

Yes.  

Not every lightning strike leaves dramatic visible damage. In some situations, damage may be limited to specific roofing components or hidden areas of the home.  

After a severe thunderstorm, watch for signs such as: 

  • Missing or damaged shingles 
  • Loose flashing 
  • Water stains on ceilings or walls 
  • Burn marks around vents or roofing materials 
  • Electrical issues that begin immediately after the storm 

If you believe your home may have been affected by lightning, document any visible damage and contact your insurance company for guidance. 

What Should You Do After a Lightning Strike? 

If your home may have been struck by lightning, consider these steps: 

  • Make sure everyone is safe 
  • Call emergency services immediately if there is a fire, smoke, or another emergency 
  • Take photos or videos of any visible damage when it is safe to do so 
  • Avoid handling damaged electrical equipment until it has been evaluated 
  • Contact your insurance company to report the loss and discuss next steps 
  • Take reasonable steps to help prevent additional damage if it is safe to do so 

What Should You Do After a Lightning Strike? 

While no home can be completely protected from lightning, there are steps homeowners can take to help reduce potential damage.

The National Weather Service recommends staying indoors during thunderstorms and waiting at least 30 minutes after hearing the last clap of thunder before going back outside. The agency’s safety message is simple: “When thunder roars, go indoors.”   

Additional ways to help protect your home include: 

  • Install whole-home surge protection if appropriate for your home 
  • Use surge protectors for valuable electronics 
  • Keep trees around your home properly maintained 
  • Stay current on routine roof and exterior maintenance 
  • Review your homeowners insurance coverage before severe weather season 

Stay Prepared Before the Next Storm 

Lightning is one of nature’s most powerful forces, and even a single strike can affect multiple areas of your home. Understanding the potential risks and reviewing your homeowners insurance coverage before severe weather arrives can help you feel more prepared when storms are in the forecast.

Tornado Safety Tips and Insurance Information for Homeowners

Tornadoes can develop with little warning, bringing powerful winds that can damage homes, vehicles, trees, and personal belongings. Whiles tornadoes are most commonly associated with the Great Plains, they also occur throughout the Southeast, including Florida, Georgia, Alabama, the Carolinas, and Tennessee.  

For homeowners, severe weather preparedness means more than knowing where to take shelter. Understanding how tornadoes form, what damage they can cause, how homeowners insurance may respond to covered losses, and what to do after a storm can help you make informed decisions before sever weather strikes.  

What is a Tornado? 

A tornado is a violently rotating column of air extending from a thunderstorm to the ground. The strongest tornadoes can produce wind exceeding 200 mph, causing damage to homes, vehicles, trees, and infrastructure.  

Although tornadoes can occur during any month of the year, they are most common during periods of active severe weather. According to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Association (NOAA), staying informed about changing weather conditions is one of the best ways homeowners can prepare.  

Tornado Watch vs. Tornado Warning 

Understanding weather alerts can help you respond quickly when severe weather develops.  

Tornado Watch: Be Prepared 

A Tornado Watch means conditions are favorable for tornadoes to develop. It does not mean a tornado has been confirmed.  

During a tornado watch: 

  • Monitor weather updates 
  • Charge your phone and backup batteries 
  • Review your family’s emergency plan 
  • Identify your safest shelter location 
  • Secure outdoor furniture and loose items if it is safe to do so 

Tornado watches are issued by NOAA’S Storm Prediction Center and may cover multiple counties or states.  

Tornado Warning: Take Shelter Immediately 

A Tornado Warning means a tornado has been observed by trained spotters or indicated by weather radar.  

If a tornado warning is issued: 

  • Move immediately to your designated shelter 
  • Stay away from windows and exterior walls 
  • Continue to monitoring official weather alerts until the warning expires 

Warnings are issued by local National Weather Service offices when immediate protective action is needed.  

How a Tornado Forms 

According to NOAA, most tornadoes develop from supercell thunderstorms, a rotating type of thunderstorm responsible for many of the strongest tornadoes.  

Tornado formation often begins with wind shear, where winds at different heights move at different speeds or directions. This created horizontal rotation in the atmosphere. Powerful updrafts within a thunderstorm can tilt that rotation vertically, and under the right atmospheric conditions, the rotating column may extend downward until it reaches the ground as a tornado.  

Understanding how tornadoes form helps explain why weather experts may issue tornado watches before a tornado develops. 

Common Types of Tornado Damage to Home 

A tornado’s impact extends beyond the roof. High winds, flying debris, and fallen trees can affect multiple parts of your property.  

Common types of tornado damage include: 

  • Roof damage, including missing shingles or structural damage 
  • Broken windows and damaged exterior doors 
  • Siding, gutters and soffit damage 
  • Fallen trees damaging the home or other structures 
  • Damage to detached garages, shed, and fences 
  • Water damage that occurs after wind creates openings in the home 
  • Damage to personal belongings inside the home from a covered loss 

The amount and type of damage depend on the tornado's strength, home construction, and where the storm travels.  

What is Tornado Insurance? 

Despite the name, there generally isn’t a separate tornado insurance policy.  

Instead, tornado damage is often covered under a standard homeowners insurance policy as wind damage, subject to the policy’s terms, conditions, exclusions, deductibles, and endorsements.  

How Homeowners Insurance May Respond to Tornado Damage 

If your home experiences damage from a covered tornado-related loss, homeowners insurance may provide coverage for several types of damage, depending on your policy. 

Coverage may include: 

  • Dwelling Coverage – May help cover damage to the home’s structure, including the roof, walls, attached garage, and other built-in features. 
  • Other Structures Coverage – May help cover detached structures, such as detached garages, sheds or fences, if covered by your policy.  
  • Personal Property Coverage – May help cover furniture, clothing, electronics, and other belongings damaged by a covered loss.  
  • Additional Living Expenses – If a covered loss makes your home temporarily uninhabitable, your policy may provide coverage for additional living expenses, subject to policy limits and terms.  

Coverage varies by policy. Review your policy documents or speak with your insurance professional to better understand your specific coverage.  

What is a Wind or Wind/Hail Deductible? 

Some homeowners insurance policies include a separate wind or wind/hail deductible that may apply when damage results from covered wind events, including tornadoes.  

Depending on your policy, the deductible may be: 

  • A fixed dollar amount 
  • A percentage of your home’s insured value 

Deductibles vary by insurer, state, and policy. Reviewing your policy before severe weather season can help you understand which deductible may apply if you experience a covered loss.  

How Homeowners Can Help Protect Their Home Before a Tornado 

Preparing your property before severe weather arrives may help reduce damage and simplify recovery afterward.  

Consider these steps: 

  • Inspect your roof for loose or damaged shingles 
  • Trim dead or overhanging tree branches 
  • Secure patio furniture, grills, and outdoor decorations 
  • Keep gutters and downspouts clear 
  • Photograph your home’s interior, exterior, and valuable belongings each year 
  • Store important documents in a waterproof container or secure digital location 
  • Assemble an emergency kit with water, flashlights, batteries, medications, and first aid supplies 
  • Enable Wireless Emergency Alerts on your mobile phone 
  • Keep a NOAA Weather Radio available if possible 
  • Review your homeowners insurance policy before storm season 

Where Should You Shelter During a Tornado? 

If a tornado warning is issued or you observe signs of an approaching tornado, seek shelter immediately.  

If you’re at home – Mobile Homes are especially vulnerable during tornadoes. If time permits, move to a designated storm shelter or sturdy building before severe weather reaches your location.  

If you’re driving – driving during a tornado can be extremely dangerous.  

If possible:  

  • Seek shelter inside a sturdy building 
  • Follow instructions from local emergency officials 
  • Avoid attempting to outrun a tornado in heavy traffic or unfamiliar areas 

Always follow guidance from the National Weather Service and local emergency management officials.  

Filing a Tornado Insurance Claim 

After ensuring everyone is safe and emergency officials have said it is safe to return, take these steps if your home has been damaged: 

  • Document damage with photos and videos before beginning cleanup, if it is safe to do so. 
  • Take reasonable temporary steps to help prevent additional damage when it can be done safely. 
  • Save receipts for emergency repairs and temporary protective measures. 
  • Separate damaged property from undamaged belongings when possible. 
  • Contact your insurance company promptly to report the loss. 
  • Work with your claims representative and provide any requested documentation during the claims process. 

Coverage depends on the specific terms, conditions, exclusions, deductibles, and endorsements of your homeowners insurance policy. 

Frequently Asked Questions 

Does homeowners insurance cover tornado damage? 
Many homeowners insurance policies generally provide coverage for tornado damage caused by wind, subject to the policy’s terms, conditions, exclusions, deductibles, and endorsements. Review your policy or speak with your insurance professional to better understand your coverage. 

Is roof damage from a tornado covered? 
Coverage for roof damage depends on the cause of the loss and your specific policy. Contact your insurance company promptly if your roof is damaged following severe weather. 

Are fallen trees covered by homeowners insurance? 
If a tree falls because of a covered peril and damages insured property, coverage may be available depending on your policy. Coverage varies by insurer and policy. 

Does homeowners insurance cover flooding after a tornado? 
Not necessarily. Standard homeowners insurance policies typically do not cover flood damage. If flooding occurs after a tornado, separate flood insurance may be needed for flood-related losses, depending on the cause of the damage and your policy. Review your policy documents or speak with your insurance professional to better understand your coverage. 

What’s the difference between a tornado watch and a tornado warning? 
A tornado watch means conditions are favorable for tornadoes to develop. A tornado warning means a tornado has been observed or indicated by weather radar, and you should seek shelter immediately. 

How can homeowners prepare for tornado season? 
Maintaining your property, creating an emergency plan, monitoring weather alerts, documenting your belongings, assembling an emergency kit, and reviewing your homeowners insurance coverage before severe weather season can help you prepare. 

Stay Prepared Before Severe Weather Strikes 

Tornadoes can develop quickly, making preparation one of the most important steps homeowners can take before severe weather occurs. Understanding weather alerts, maintaining your property, and having an emergency plan can help reduce risk and improve your ability to respond when storms threaten. 

It’s also important to review your homeowners insurance policy before severe weather season so you understand how your coverage may respond if your home experiences a covered loss. Because every policy is different, reviewing your policy documents or speaking with your insurance professional ahead of time can help you make informed decisions before severe weather arrives.

Why Florida Homes Have Pool Enclosures: Benefits, Building Requirements, and Safety Considerations

If you’ve spent time in Florida neighborhoods, you’ve probably noticed that many backyard pools are surrounded by screened enclosures, often called pool cages. While these structures can help keep out insects and debris, they can also play an important role in helping homeowners meet pool barrier requirements and support a layered approach to pool safety.  

Whether you’re installing a new pool or updating your backyard, understanding why pool enclosures are so common in Florida can help you make informed decisions for your home. Here’s what homeowners should know about pool enclosures, Florida pool barrier requirements, and additional safety considerations.  

Why do Florida Pools Have Cages? 

Screened pool enclosures are a familiar feature throughout Florida. Many homeowners choose them because they can help reduce insects, leaves, pollen, and other debris from entering the pool while allowing for ventilation and outdoor enjoyment. 

Depending on your location, a properly installed pool enclosure or barrier may also help homeowners meet applicable local building code requirements. According to Florida’s residential building code, most residential swimming pools must be protected by an approved safety barrier that meets specific requirements for height, gates, and access. 

Whether you have a screened enclosure or another type of approved barrier, the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) recommends viewing barriers as one layer of a comprehensive pool safety plan – not a replacement for supervision. 

Florida Pool Barrier Requirements 

Florida law generally requires new residential swimming pools to have an approved safety barrier. Depending on the property, this may include a screened enclosure, safety fence, self-closing gates, or other approved barrier systems that meet applicable building code requirements. 

Requirements may vary depending on the age of the home, local jurisdiction, and the type of barrier installed. Homeowners planning to build or modify a pool should review local requirements and work with qualified professionals to help ensure compliance.  

Benefits of Pool Enclosures 

Helps Keep Insects Out 

Florida’s climate makes mosquitoes and other insects common throughout much of the year. A screened enclosure can help create a more comfortable outdoor space.  

Reduces Debris 

Leaves, pollen, and small branches are less likely to enter the pool, helping reduce routine cleaning.  

Supports Pool Safety 

When designed and installed to meet applicable requirements, approved barriers can help reduce unsupervised access to the pool.  

Creates More Useable Outdoor Space 

Many homeowners use screened enclosures to enjoy outdoor living while reducing direct sun exposure and wind-blown debris.  

Pool Enclosures and Homeowners Insurance 

Swimming pools are an attractive feature for many homes, but they can also increase potential liability exposures. Installing and maintaining appropriate safety features, such as approved barriers, may help reduce certain risks around the property.  

Coverage, underwriting requirements, and eligibility vary by insurer and policy. Homeowners should review their coverage and discuss any questions with their insurance professional.  

Types of Florida Pool Enclosures 

Florida homeowners have several options when choosing a pool enclosure or barrier. The best choice depends on your property’s layout, local building requirements, maintenance preferences, and how you use your outdoor living space.  

Screen Enclosures (Pool Cages) 

A screened pool enclosure, commonly called a pool cage, is one of the most recognizable features of Florida homes. These structures help reduce insects, leaves, pollen, and other debris from entering the pool while allowing fresh air and natural light. Many homeowners also appreciate the added comfort and reduced maintenance they provide.  

Safety Fences 

Removable or permanent safety fences can help limit unsupervised access to the pool. Many designs include self-closing, self-latching gates, and are commonly used as part of a layered pool safety approach.  

Permanent Barriers 

Walls, fences, and other approved permanent barriers may help homeowners meet applicable Florida pool barrier requirements when designed and installed in accordance with local building codes.  

Combination Systems 

Some homeowners choose to combine a screened enclosure with additional safety features, such as self-closing gates, alarms, or safety fencing, to create multiple layers of protection.  

Before installing or modifying a pool enclosure or barrier, homeowners should review applicable local building code requirements and work with qualified professionals to help ensure the installation complies with all regulations.  

Pool Enclosures Are Just One Layer of Protection 

A pool enclosure is an important safety feature, but it should be viewed as one part of a broader pool safety plan. According to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission’s Pool Safely campaign, homeowners can help reduce risk by combining multiple layers of protection, including: 

  • Installing and maintaining an approved pool barrier or enclosure with self-closing, self-latching gates where required 
  • Providing constant, active adult supervision whenever children or pets are in or around the water 
  • Removing toys and flotation devices from the pool area after use to discourage unsupervised access 
  • Learning CPR and having an emergency plan in place, recognizing that preparation can be valuable during an emergency 

No single safety measure can eliminate the risk of drowning. Combining physical barriers with supervision and emergency preparedness helps create a safer backyard environment.  

Final Thoughts

Screened pool enclosures have become a defining feature of many Florida homes for good reason. Beyond helping reduce insects and debris, they may assist homeowners in meeting applicable pool barrier requirements while supporting a layered approach to backyard safety.  

Whether you’re planning a new pool or updating an existing one, understanding Florida’s pool barrier requirements and maintaining appropriate safety measures that can help protect your home and everyone who enjoys it. Homeowners should review local building codes and consult qualified professionals before installing or modifying a pool enclosure.

Does Homeowners Insurance Cover My College Student? What Parents Should Know

Sending a child to college comes with a long checklist – tuition, housing, meal plans, textbooks, and technology. One item that often gets overlooked is insurance. 

Many parents assume their homeowners insurance automatically covers everything once their student moves away. In reality, coverage depends on where your student lives, what type of policy you have, and the specific terms of your coverage. 

Here’s what homeowners should know before move-in day. 

When a Student Lives in a Dorm 

If your child lives in a college-owned residence hall, their personal belongings may be covered under your homeowners insurance policy while they are away at school. 

Covered property may include items such as: 

  • Clothing 
  • Furniture 
  • Laptops and tablets 
  • Televisions 
  • Small appliances 
  • Textbooks 
  • Bicycles 

However, many policies place limits on off-premises personal property coverage, meaning the amount available away from your home may be less than the total personal property limit shown on your policy. Some policies also have age, dependency, or full-time student requirements. 

What If They Live Off Campus? 

Once a student moves into an apartment, rental house, or other off-campus residence, homeowners insurance may no longer provide the same protection for their belongings. 

In many situations, renters insurance is the more appropriate option because it can help protect a student’s personal property and may also provide personal liability coverage, subject to the terms and conditions of the policy. 

Keep in mind that a landlord’s insurance policy typically protects the building – not the tenant’s personal belongings.  

What Personal Property May Be Covered? 

If coverage applies, homeowners insurance generally helps protect personal belongings against covered causes of loss, such as: 

  • Theft 
  • Fire 
  • Smoke damage 
  • Certain types of vandalism 
  • Some weather-related events covered by the policy 

Coverage depends on the cause of loss and your specific policy language. Not every type of damage is covered. 

High-Value Items May Need Additional Coverage 

Many college students bring expensive electronics and specialty items to school. 

Examples include: 

  • Gaming computers 
  • Professional cameras 
  • Musical instruments 
  • Jewelry 
  • High-end bicycles 

These items may have coverage limits under a standard homeowners policy. If your student is bringing valuable belongings to campus, ask your insurance professional whether additional coverage may be appropriate for those items. 

Don’t Forget About Your Deductible 

Even if your student’s belongings are covered, remember that your homeowners deductible still applies. 

For smaller losses, the value of the damaged or stolen property may not exceed your deductible. Reviewing your deductible before move-in can help you better understand what your policy may pay following a covered loss. 

Create a College Inventory 

Before your student leaves for school, take a few minutes to document what they’re bringing. 

Consider: 

  • Photographing valuable belongings 
  • Saving receipts when available 
  • Recording serial numbers for electronics 
  • Creating a list of major items and their approximate value 

Having an inventory can make the claims process easier if a covered loss occurs.  

Questions to Ask Before Move-In 

Before classes begin, consider asking your insurance professional: 

  • Does my homeowners policy cover my college student? 
  • Does coverage change if they live off campus? 
  • What are my off-premises personal property limits? 
  • Are expensive electronics or jewelry subject to special limits? 
  • Would renters insurance be appropriate for my student’s living situation? 

Understanding your coverage before move-in day can help prevent unexpected surprises later. 

Frequently Asked Questions 

Does homeowners insurance cover my child’s laptop at college? 
It may. If your student qualifies for coverage under your homeowners policy and the loss is caused by a covered event, their laptop may be covered, subject to your policy’s limits, exclusions, and deductible. Review your policy for details.  

Does my student need renters insurance? 
It depends on where they live and how your homeowners policy applies. Students living off campus often benefit from having renters insurance, but coverage needs vary.  

Does my homeowners policy cover everything my student owns? 
Not necessarily. Policies may include limits for off-premises property and certain categories of valuable belongings. Review your policy to understand your available coverage.  

Is theft covered in a college dorm? 
Theft may be covered if your homeowners policy extends coverage to your student’s belongings and the loss meets the policy’s requirements. Coverage varies by policy and is subject to applicable deductibles and exclusions. 

Final Thoughts 

Heading to college is an exciting milestone, and it’s also a good time to review your insurance coverage. Whether your student is moving into a dorm or renting an apartment, understanding how your homeowners policy works can help you make informed decisions before move-in day. 

Every homeowners policy is different. Always review your policy and consult your insurance professional to determine what coverage applies to your family’s specific situation.

Step Into Savings: Understanding Frontline’s Stepdown Deductible® Policy

For homeowners in areas vulnerable to hurricanes, tropical storms, and severe wind events, preparing for storm season means more than protecting your home—it also means understanding your insurance deductible. 

Frontline’s Stepdown Deductible® Policy is an optional policy designed to help eligible homeowners reduce the financial impact of a covered hurricane or wind and hail deductible over time through a deductible reimbursement benefit. 

If you’re wondering how the Stepdown Deductible® Policy works and whether it’s right for your home, here’s what you need to know. 

What Is the Stepdown Deductible® Policy? 

The Stepdown Deductible® Policy is a separate insurance policy that works alongside eligible Frontline homeowners or dwelling policies. 

Rather than changing the deductible on your homeowners policy, the Stepdown Deductible® Policy may provide reimbursement for a portion—or, after enough consecutive claim-free years, up to 100%—of your applicable hurricane or wind and hail deductible following a covered loss, subject to the policy’s terms and conditions. 

The reimbursement benefit increases with consecutive eligible claim-free years. 

How the Stepdown Deductible® Policy Works 

Eligible reimbursement increases over time for policyholders who remain claim-free for qualifying losses. 

Current reimbursement schedule: 

Year 1: Up to 5% 
Year 2: Up to 20% 
Year 3: Up to 40% 
Year 4: Up to 60% 
Year 5: Up to 80% 
Year 6: Up to 100% 

To qualify, covered losses must satisfy the policy requirements, including applicable reporting deadlines and deductible requirements. The Stepdown Deductible® Policy is purchased separately from your homeowners insurance policy and has its own premium. 

Coverage is subject to all applicable terms, conditions, exclusions, deductibles, and limits. 

Why a Deductible Reimbursement Benefit Matters 

Hurricane and wind and hail deductibles are often calculated differently than standard homeowners deductibles and may represent a significant out-of-pocket expense following a covered loss. 

The Stepdown Deductible® Policy helps reward long-term policyholders by increasing the potential reimbursement benefit after consecutive eligible claim-free years, helping reduce the financial impact of a covered deductible when a loss occurs. 

Why Homeowners Choose the Stepdown Deductible® Policy 

Depending on your situation, the Stepdown Deductible® Policy may appeal to homeowners who: 

  • Want additional financial protection following covered storm losses 
  • Live in areas susceptible to hurricanes or severe wind events 
  • Prefer to plan ahead for potential out-of-pocket expenses 
  • Value a benefit that grows over time with consecutive eligible claim-free year 

Frequently Asked Questions 

Does the Stepdown Deductible® Policy reduce my homeowners insurance deductible? 
No. The Stepdown Deductible® Policy is a separate insurance policy that may reimburse a portion of your applicable deductible following a covered loss, subject to eligibility and policy terms. 

Is the Stepdown Deductible® Policy included with my homeowners insurance? 
No. It is purchased separately and has its own premium. 

What types of losses qualify? 
Eligible reimbursement generally applies to covered hurricane or wind and hail losses, depending on your policy and state. Refer to your policy documents for complete eligibility requirements. 

How do I qualify for the maximum reimbursement? 
The reimbursement percentage increases after consecutive eligible claim-free years, up to the maximum benefit available under the policy.
